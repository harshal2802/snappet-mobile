# WiFi Senses Motion, Not Pictures

WiFi can serve as a whole-home motion tripwire today, but it cannot serve as a camera: commodity WiFi reliably answers "did something move in this space?" and almost nothing beyond that outside a laboratory. The technique reads channel state information (CSI), a per-subcarrier amplitude and phase measurement of how radio waves bounce around a room, and flags the disturbance a moving body causes. The famous "see through walls" skeleton demos came from custom radar, not WiFi chips, and the commodity-WiFi pose and imaging papers work only in the room where a camera-supervised model was trained. Commercial products from Comcast, Verizon, Plume and Signify deliver coarse zone-level motion alerts using proprietary algorithms; IEEE 802.11bf was published in September 2025, yet the research found no confirmed compliant consumer product as of November 2026. A DIY builder can reproduce the commercial capability for roughly $10 per room with an ESP32-C6 running the open-source ESPectre firmware into Home Assistant. The limits that matter are soft zone boundaries, false triggers from pets, fans and curtains, blindness to people who sit still, and a single point of failure in the WiFi link itself. The sound design treats CSI as one corroborating layer beside door contacts and PIR or mmWave sensors, processes everything locally, and stays at motion detection rather than identification. One caveat on the evidence: the researchers could not open most primary pages in their sessions, so many figures below come from search-result summaries of the cited sources and deserve a spot check before being quoted as hard fact.

## A moving body perturbs multipath, and CSI measures the perturbation

Every WiFi receiver already estimates the radio channel to decode packets. The older signal-strength reading (RSSI) collapses that estimate into one power number per packet, whereas **CSI reports amplitude and phase for each OFDM subcarrier**, which is why CSI resolves fine motion and RSSI supports only coarse presence ([survey, PMC9375645](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9375645)). The sensitivity is real: breathing shows up because chest movement changes a reflected path length by roughly **5-12 mm** ([RespirFi](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20397v1)). It is also uneven. Detection depends on where a person stands relative to the Fresnel zones between one specific transmitter and receiver, working well mid-zone and "terribly at the boundaries" ([PMC8157398](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157398)), and amplitude-only sensing has outright blind spots ([SA-WiSense](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17623v2)). The hardware adds its own distortion: a 2026 study attributes much cross-device accuracy loss to receiver gain control and subcarrier nonlinearities ([arXiv 2605.26836](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26836)).

The "camera" framing comes from a set of landmark results that are routinely misattributed. WiSee (2013) classified nine gestures at 94% accuracy through walls, but on USRP software-defined radios ([WiSee](https://wisee.cs.washington.edu/)). MIT's RF-Capture and RF-Pose produced through-wall figures and skeletons using a purpose-built FMCW radar at WiFi-like frequencies, not a WiFi chipset; RF-Pose scored **58.1 AP through a wall** against 62.4 in line of sight ([RF-Pose paper](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mingmin/papers/rfpose-cvpr-zhao.pdf); [project page](https://rfpose.csail.mit.edu/)). The pattern across the literature, in the researchers' synthesis, is that the more camera-like the output, the more specialised the radio or the more controlled the room.

Commodity-WiFi results are genuine but narrow. CMU's DensePose from WiFi reached AP 43.5 under a same-layout protocol using two three-antenna routers placed on opposite sides of the subjects, and its authors concede performance is limited "especially under different layouts" ([arXiv 2301.00250](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00250); [ISPreview](https://www.ispreview.co.uk/index.php/2023/01/scientists-find-way-of-using-wifi-to-monitor-people-through-walls.html)). Person-in-WiFi 3D (CVPR 2024) reports joint errors of **91.7 mm for one person rising to 125.3 mm for three**, inside a 4 m x 3.5 m area with seven volunteers ([CVPR 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/html/Yan_Person-in-WiFi_3D_End-to-End_Multi-Person_3D_Pose_Estimation_with_Wi-Fi_CVPR_2024_paper.html)). LatentCSI (2025) generates photorealistic room images from CSI, but the detail comes from a pretrained diffusion model trained on real photos of that same room, with CSI steering the generation ([arXiv 2506.10605](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10605); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/wi-fi-signals-can-now-create-accurate-images-of-a-room-with-the-help-of-pre-trained-ai-latentcsi-leverages-stable-diffusion-3-to-turn-wi-fi-data-into-a-digital-paintbrush)). Identification has advanced fastest: WhoFi re-identified people at 95.5% Rank-1 among 14 subjects ([arXiv 2507.12869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12869)), and KIT's BFId reached 99.5% among 197 people using beamforming feedback rather than CSI ([KIT](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/pi_2025_069_the-spy-who-came-in-from-the-wifi-beware-of-radio-network-surveillance.php)). Both are closed-set results; neither shows the ability to flag an unknown intruder as "not a household member", and no independent replication was found.

The research frontier in 2025-2026 has moved from new headline capabilities to generalization, which is itself the tell. A 2026 survey of more than 200 papers frames failure on new users, devices and rooms as the core problem ([arXiv 2503.08008](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.08008v4)). One practicality study watched two models fall from **above 85% to roughly 30%** in a different environment ([arXiv 2409.07495](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.07495v1)). Pretraining on 1.3 million CSI samples from 14 datasets bought only 2.2 to 15.7 points of cross-domain accuracy, with the authors concluding that data, not model size, is the bottleneck ([arXiv 2511.18792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18792v1)). The largest multimodal pose dataset, MM-Fi, has 40 subjects ([arXiv 2305.10345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10345v2)), and the researchers found no public dataset covering home intrusion with pets and appliances across real homes.

## Shipping products deliver zone-level motion, and the standard has not reached shelves

The commercial market confirms the research verdict. ABI Research states that most WiFi sensing solutions "only provide simple presence detection" ([ABI Research](https://go.abiresearch.com/hubfs/Marketing/Whitepapers/The%20Commercial%20Market%20Value%20for%20Wi-Fi%20Sensing/ABi_Research_The_Commercial_Market_Value_for_WiFi_Sensing.pdf)). Products watch the link between a router and stationary connected devices and report motion without identity or location ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/113538-comcast-adds-home-motion-sensing-millions-xfinity-routers.html)).

The most telling market signal is who is buying. **ADT acquired Origin AI for $170 million in February 2026** and expects to commercialise offerings in 2027, pitching the technology as a way to add context and reduce false alarms inside a monitored system rather than as a standalone sensor ([ADT newsroom](https://newsroom.adt.com/corporate-news/adt-acquires-origin-ai-to-power-ai-sensing-and-ambient-intelligence-for-the-home)). Standalone consumer WiFi-sensing security products, by contrast, did not last: the paid Linksys Aware subscription ended, and the surviving model is a free feature bundled into a service people already pay for. Independent testing is thin and qualitative; the researchers found no third-party measurement of detection rate, latency or false alarms for any current product. Market-size estimates vary by an order of magnitude and come from low-transparency vendors; the more credible indicator is ABI's forecast of **112 million sensing-capable WiFi devices installed in North America by 2030** ([ABI Research](https://abiresearch.com/press/north-american-wi-fi-sensing-cpe-installations-to-surge-to-112-million-by-2030-as-the-technologys-maturing-unleashes-new-business-and-service-models)).

IEEE Std 802.11bf-2025 was approved on 28 May 2025 and published on 26 September 2025, covering licence-exempt bands from 1 to 7.125 GHz and above 45 GHz ([standard front matter](https://people.freebsd.org/~adrian/net80211/80211bf-2025.pdf)). It standardises how devices advertise sensing capability, request sounding transmissions and exchange measurement reports ([arXiv 2310.17661](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.17661)); detection algorithms are explicitly out of scope ([arXiv 2212.13930](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.13930)). The practical effect will be interoperability, ending today's dependence on vendor compatibility lists, not better accuracy. CableLabs confirmed in May 2026 that the products being deployed are "legacy" pre-standard ones ([CableLabs](https://www.cablelabs.com/blog/how-wireless-networks-are-learning-to-sense-the-world-around-them)), and no chipset or product was confirmed compliant. Whether a Wi-Fi Alliance certification programme for sensing has launched could not be verified.

## Motion detection is deployable; everything above it degrades fast

The capabilities sort into a ladder, and a home-security builder should plan around the bottom two rungs. The ranking below is the researchers' synthesis of the cited literature.

| Capability | Realism on commodity WiFi | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-home motion detection | Deployable now | Shipped on millions of devices; 92.61% overall accuracy in one large-scale deployment paper ([arXiv 2506.04322](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04322v1)) |
| Human versus pet, room-level zoning | Works with engineering effort | False alarms from non-human motion cut from **63.1% to 8.4%** in the same paper |
| Stationary presence via breathing | Position-dependent | Blind spots and location sensitivity ([RespirFi](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20397v1)) |
| Fall detection | Too many false alarms for unattended use | 8-18% false-alarm rates in WiFall and RT-Fall ([arXiv 1901.00715](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.00715); [RT-Fall](https://shengjieli-pku.github.io/ShengjieLi/Paper/RT-FALL.pdf)) |
| Identification | Closed-set lab results only | WhoFi, BFId |
| Pose and "imaging" | Laboratory only | Same-room training, dedicated multi-antenna links |

Published intrusion-detection figures look excellent: WiBorder reports 99.4% detection with 0.68% false alarms ([WiBorder](https://shengjieli-pku.github.io/ShengjieLi/Paper/WiBorder.pdf)), WiDetect claims 99.68% with zero false rate ([WiDetect](https://ct.cswu.me/papers/ubicomp19_widetect_paper.pdf)), and Origin's own slides give 96.67% detection with 1.92% false alarms while naming pets and appliances as a major false-alarm source ([WiSense 2025 slides](https://ebulutvcu.github.io/WiSense25/Guozhen_Zhu.pdf)). These are self-reported results from the authors' own setups, and no independent benchmark exists. A per-trial false-alarm rate of 1-2% also says little about alerts per week in an occupied home; the 63.1%-to-8.4% deployment figure is the most realistic number found, and it still leaves roughly one non-human motion event in twelve misclassified.

Four limitations shape any design. First, **zone boundaries are soft**. The through-wall penetration that gives one link whole-home coverage also registers motion in adjacent rooms; WiBorder exists precisely to confine detection to a walled room, and the TOMMY project warns that a zone "may also detect presence outside the intended zone" ([tommysense.com](https://tommysense.com/)). Second, nuisance sources are everywhere: ESPectre's tuning guide lists fans, AC units, moving curtains and pets ([ESPectre tuning](https://espectre.dev/documentation/tuning/)). No quantified study of fans or curtains was found, so their real-world rate is unknown. Third, motion detectors go blind to still people; ESPectre "will eventually report 'no motion'" for someone sitting still, like a PIR ([Home Assistant community](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/espectre-wi-fi-motion-detection-for-home-assistant/961251)), and earlier intrusion detectors lose accuracy against very slow movement ([Stevens Institute](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/publications/robust-wlan-based-indoor-intrusion-detection-using-phy-layer-info/)). Fourth, performance depends on network topology: WiZ concedes that mesh networks and repeaters degrade its sensing ([WiZ FAQ](https://faq.wizconnected.com/hc/cs/7-wiz-v2/faq/488-what-do-i-need-to-know-about-spacesense)), and Comcast says home layout, materials and device placement all affect detection and guarantees nothing ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/comcast-xfinity-shield-wifi-motion-privacy)).

Against the incumbents, WiFi sensing is neither cheaper nor more reliable per room. An LD2410C 24 GHz radar module costs about $3-4.50 and detects a motionless, breathing person ([LCSC](https://www.lcsc.com/product-detail/C19723500.html); [openelab](https://openelab.io/blogs/learn/24ghz-vs-60ghz-mmwave-presence-sensors)). One hobbyist CSI build reached about 80% agreement with a commercial 24 GHz radar ([Hackster](https://www.hackster.io/limengdu0117/esp-csi-diy-wifi-human-presence-detection-f80508)). CSI earns its place where its unique properties count: no line of sight, coverage through walls and furniture, hidden or software-only deployment, and no camera. It fits best as an "armed-away, the house should be empty" tripwire, where any motion is meaningful and stillness-blindness is irrelevant, provided no pets, fans or robot vacuums are active.

## A $10 ESP32 per room reproduces the commercial capability

The ESP32 family is the only low-cost, in-production, well-supported CSI platform. Espressif states that all ESP32 series support CSI, recommends an external antenna over the directional on-board one, and notes that a dedicated packet sender on a fixed channel gives the highest accuracy, ahead of the simpler approach of pinging the home router ([espressif/esp-csi](https://github.com/espressif/esp-csi)). Its CSI-quality ranking runs **ESP32-C5 > C6 > C3 ≈ S3 > classic ESP32** ([Espressif docs](https://documentation.espressif.com/esp-csi/master/README.md)). The step-up options suit research, not deployment: a Raspberry Pi with Nexmon CSI captures up to 80 MHz but ties up the Pi's WiFi in monitor mode and is kernel-sensitive ([nexmon_csi](https://github.com/seemoo-lab/nexmon_csi)), while Intel AX200/AX210 cards with the open-source FeitCSI reach 160 MHz and the 6 GHz band but need a Linux PC per receiver ([FeitCSI](https://github.com/KuskoSoft/FeitCSI)). The Intel 5300 that underpins much of the literature is discontinued ([arXiv 2305.10554](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.10554)).

On software, popularity is a poor guide. **ESPectre** (9.8k stars) is the recommended starting point: training-free motion detection running entirely on-device, supporting the C6, C5, C3, S3, S2 and classic ESP32 against an ordinary 2.4 GHz network, and integrating through ESPHome, MQTT Discovery or Matter ([ESPectre](https://github.com/francescopace/espectre); [ESPHome component](https://esphome.io/components/espectre/)). It states its own limits plainly: it "does not identify people, count them, prove that a room is empty, or replace a safety-certified security, medical, or emergency system." Espressif's esp-csi and esp-radar provide the official SDK and an on-device motion and presence demo for custom firmware. SenseFi is the standard PyTorch benchmark for offline experiments ([SenseFi](https://github.com/xyanchen/WiFi-CSI-Sensing-Benchmark)).

The viral **RuView / "WiFi DensePose" repository (55k+ stars) should not be relied on**. Its own README concedes that the Docker image runs on simulated data, that an earlier "100% presence" figure was retracted, and that its live single-ESP32 pose model scores **PCK@20 of 3.0%** ([RuView README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ruvnet/RuView/main/README.md)). The maintainer wrote that the detection pipeline is heuristic thresholds rather than a trained model and that "the 3D skeleton you see in the UI is a visualization driven by CSI heuristics, not a true DensePose reconstruction" ([issue #299](https://github.com/ruvnet/RuView/issues/299)). Its strong published number is an offline benchmark on the MM-Fi academic dataset, not a live ESP32 result.

Working detectors use a small classical pipeline rather than deep learning. ESPectre computes the spread of amplitudes across subcarriers per packet, tracks a moving variance of that value, selects a 12-subcarrier band during a quiet-room calibration of about 7.5 seconds at boot, and applies an adaptive threshold ([Hacker News, developer comment](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45958166); [ESPectre tuning](https://espectre.dev/documentation/tuning/)). Hampel outlier filtering is the standard first clean-up step ([arXiv 2507.12854](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.12854)). Cited packet rates range from 20 to 100 per second depending on firmware version, and ESPectre's guidance is one board per room.

A recommended path follows from all of this, in four stages. Start with a weekend prototype: one ESP32-C6 with ESPectre flashed through ESPHome, exposing a motion binary sensor, a movement score and a threshold to Home Assistant, using the existing 2.4 GHz router as transmitter. Log the movement score for one to two weeks before trusting it, deliberately testing pets, fans, heating cycles and movement in adjoining rooms or flats, because no independent false-alarm data exists and your home is the only valid test set. Next, harden the radio side: add a dedicated always-on transmitter so links do not depend on the main router, fit external antennas, expand to one node per area, and trigger recalibration when the system arms and the house is confirmed empty, since the threshold is learned at boot. Then build the alarm logic as sensor fusion: require a door contact, PIR or LD2410 radar to corroborate a CSI event before sounding anything, arm CSI zones only in away mode, and treat loss of a sensing link as an alarm condition in its own right. Only after that is stable does a research rig make sense, using an Intel AX210 with FeitCSI and SenseFi-style models to explore classification, with the expectation that any model must be trained on data from your own rooms.

## The same signals leak outward, and the law has not caught up

A WiFi-sensing alarm can expose the very fact it protects. Researchers tracked people inside 11 real offices and apartments from outside using one commodity smartphone measuring only signal strength, and found that more WiFi devices inside made tracking more accurate ([Et Tu Alexa, arXiv 1810.10109](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.10109v4)). LeakyBeam detected occupancy from **20 m away with an 82.7% true-positive and 96.7% true-negative rate** using beamforming feedback that 86% of WiFi 5/6 devices send in plaintext ([NDSS 2025](https://ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2025-5-paper.pdf)). Wi-Peep located every WiFi device in a building to about a metre from a drone carrying roughly $20 of hardware ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/media/waterloo-researchers-discover-security-loophole-allowing)). WPA2 and WPA3 encryption do not help, because these attacks read physical-layer or unencrypted feedback information. No evidence of use in actual residential crime was found, but the design implication stands: use the fewest sensing links that do the job, keep them away from exterior walls, and prefer wired devices for the security-critical parts.

As an intrusion detector the technology is also attackable and fragile. Preamble-manipulation attacks altered CSI with 90.47% success against activity recognition ([NJIT/Rutgers](https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/practical-adversarial-attack-on-wifi-sensing-through-unnoticeable/)), though most published attacks target deep-learning classifiers rather than simple motion thresholds. The blunter risk is that jamming, deauthentication or a power cut disables detection across the whole home at once. Protected Management Frames, mandatory in WPA3, block classic deauthentication but not all variants, and nothing stops RF jamming ([arXiv 2602.23513](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23513v1)). Dual-technology alarm sensors cut false alarms precisely because two independent technologies must agree ([IPVM](https://ipvm.com/reports/pir-detector-selection-guide)), which is the same logic behind fusing CSI with a second sensor.

On privacy, the claim that WiFi sensing is a benign alternative to cameras is true only in the narrow sense that it produces no images. The 2025 identification results show the same signals work as biometric identifiers, and the KIT researchers called explicitly for privacy safeguards in 802.11bf ([KIT](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/pi_2025_069_the-spy-who-came-in-from-the-wifi-beware-of-radio-network-surveillance.php)). Cloud-held data carries a separate risk: Comcast's terms, as quoted by Bruce Schneier, allow it to disclose WiFi Motion information "to third parties without further notice to you in connection with any law enforcement investigation or proceeding" ([Schneier on Security](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2026/09/wireless-routers-as-motion-detectors.html)), and disabling the feature reportedly does not delete past motion logs ([All About Cookies](https://allaboutcookies.org/comcast-motion-sensor-routers)). A locally processed DIY detector avoids that exposure entirely.

No statute or court ruling found addresses CSI sensing directly, so legality rests on analogy, and what follows is general information rather than legal advice. Motion sensing confined to your own home for personal purposes resembles domestic CCTV, which UK and Irish regulators treat as within the household exemption only while capture stays inside the property boundary ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/domestic-cctv-using-cctv-systems-on-your-property/domestic-cctv-using-cctv-systems-on-your-property); [Irish DPC](https://www.dataprotection.ie/sites/default/files/uploads/2026-05/Domestic_CCTV_A5_ENG_Final.pdf)); the EU's Ryneš ruling removed that exemption for a camera covering a neighbour's property ([Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/cctv-cjeu-narrows-the-scope-of-the-household-exemption)). Radio does not stop at a party wall and cannot be re-aimed like a lens, so flats and terraced houses carry the most risk. Identification raises the stakes further: California's CCPA expressly lists "gait patterns or rhythms" as biometric information ([Troutman Pepper](https://www.troutman.com/insights/are-you-ready-for-the-bipa-tsunami-the-new-wave-of-biometric-statutes/)), although Illinois BIPA's closed list omits gait ([Morrison Foerster](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240503-getting-bipa-right-biometric-identifiers-must-identify)). One rule is unambiguous in the United States: jamming or deauthenticating other people's devices is illegal regardless of motive, as Marriott's $600,000 penalty showed ([FCC advisory](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-15-113A1_Rcd.pdf)). No WiFi-sensing-specific code of practice exists, so the prudent practice assembled from camera guidance is to tell everyone who lives in or works in the home, keep detection inside your own unit, exclude bathrooms and guests' or workers' bedrooms, store events rather than raw CSI, and stop at motion rather than identity.

## Conclusion

The useful mental model is a tripwire that fills a volume instead of crossing a doorway. That reframing explains both why the technology is finally shipping at scale and why security incumbents are buying it as a context layer rather than a sensor replacement: its value is coverage without line of sight, and its weakness is that it cannot say what moved or exactly where. A builder who accepts that trade gets a cheap, private, camera-free layer; one who chases pose or imaging gets a research project that will not leave the room it was trained in.

Two things could change the picture, and both deserve watching. Standards-compliant 802.11bf silicon would let any access point request sounding from any client, which removes the compatibility friction but also makes sensing a default capability of every network, including the neighbours'. And raw CSI is re-analysable: captures stored today can be mined later with better models, including for identification. The durable design principle is therefore to keep capability and data at the minimum the security goal needs, because in this field the sensing hardware stays the same while what software can infer from it keeps growing.
