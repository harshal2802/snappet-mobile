# WiFi Sensing for Home Security: Privacy, Legal, Ethical and Security-Robustness Considerations (state as of Nov 2026)

Scope note: general information for a residential setting, not legal advice. Research method caveat: findings come from web search result summaries; direct page fetches of primary pages (schneier.com, xfinity.com) were blocked by this session's network policy, so quotations of Comcast's terms are as relayed by secondary coverage and should be re-checked against the primary page before being quoted verbatim in a final report. Numbers from papers come from abstracts / press coverage, not full-text reading.

## 1. What privacy concerns have researchers, regulators and civil-society groups raised about WiFi sensing?

### Takeaway
The core concern is that WiFi sensing is invisible, works through walls, needs no device on the person sensed, and has moved from "motion/presence" toward person identification (WhoFi, BFId, both 2025) while being rolled out at ISP scale (Comcast WiFi Motion, widely covered again in Sept 2026) under terms that allow disclosure to law enforcement and other third parties. The 802.11bf sensing standard was published in Sept 2025 without the privacy question being regarded as settled by researchers.

### Cited Findings

Person identification from radio signatures
- WhoFi (Sapienza University of Rome; arXiv July 2025, revised Aug 2025) re-identifies people from WiFi CSI with a Transformer-based network; headline result about 95.5% Rank-1 accuracy on the public NTU-Fi dataset — [arXiv 2507.12869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12869); [The Register, 22 Jul 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/22/whofi_wifi_identifier/)
- Caveat on WhoFi: NTU-Fi is a small benchmark (14 subjects, controlled indoor environment); it is a preprint benchmark result, not a deployed system — [arXiv 2507.12869 (html v2)](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.12869v2)
- The WhoFi authors frame WiFi sensing as privacy-preserving relative to cameras (no images); press coverage contests this, calling device-free tracking a potential "privacy nightmare" and noting a person could be followed across WiFi networks even without carrying a phone. The paper itself says deployments should consider "legal implications of radio biometric profiling" — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/wi-fi-signals-could-be-used-to-uniquely-identify-individuals-whofi-complements-biometrics-prompting-privacy-fears); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/22/whofi_wifi_identifier/); [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/novel-ai-system-whofi-identifies-people-using-only-wi-fi-signals/)
- BFId (Todt, Morsbach, Strufe; KASTEL/KIT; ACM CCS 2025, DOI 10.1145/3719027.3765062) is described as the first identity-inference attack using beamforming feedback information (BFI) rather than CSI; evaluated on recordings from 197 participants, with "almost 100%" accuracy per KIT (Tom's Hardware reports 99.5%). Once the model is trained, identification takes a few seconds — [KIT press release 069/2025](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/pi_2025_069_the-spy-who-came-in-from-the-wifi-beware-of-radio-network-surveillance.php); [KIT publication record](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000185756); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/researchers-identify-people-through-ordinary-wi-fi-routers-with-99-percent-accuracy)
- Why BFI matters more than CSI for privacy: clients periodically send compressed channel feedback to the access point, and it is transmitted unencrypted, so a passive monitor-mode adapter can capture it without specialised hardware or even being connected to the network. The person identified need not carry any device; it is enough that WiFi devices near them communicate — [KIT press release](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/pi_2025_069_the-spy-who-came-in-from-the-wifi-beware-of-radio-network-surveillance.php); [Physics World, "Is your WiFi spying on you?"](https://physicsworld.com/a/is-your-wifi-spying-on-you/)
- The KIT researchers explicitly call for privacy safeguards in IEEE 802.11bf and warn about use by authoritarian states (e.g. monitoring protesters). Encrypting BFI would require changes to the WiFi standard and could break backward compatibility — [KIT press release](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/pi_2025_069_the-spy-who-came-in-from-the-wifi-beware-of-radio-network-surveillance.php); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1101595)

Keystroke / gesture inference
- WiKey (MobiCom 2015): keystroke recognition from CSI with two commodity WiFi devices; reported 97.5% keystroke detection, 96.4% single-key classification, 93.5% on continuously typed sentences (lab conditions) — [WiKey abstract](https://www.springerprofessional.de/doi/10.1145/2789168.2790109)
- WindTalker (ACM CCS 2016): a malicious hotspot infers phone PIN/password entry from CSI without compromising the victim device; demonstrated against Alipay PIN entry, with reported recovery rates of 68.3% (one training sample per keystroke) up to 81.7% with more samples. Analysts quoted at the time doubted real-world practicality and noted easier ways exist to steal passwords — [WindTalker paper](https://nsec.sjtu.edu.cn/publications/2016/When.pdf); [The Register, Nov 2016](https://www.theregister.co.uk/2016/11/13/researchers_point_finger_at_handy_smartphone_exploit/); [SC Media](https://www.scworld.com/wi-fi-can-imprint-passwords-and-pins-onto-radio-signals/article/573508/)
- Overviews of 802.11bf note sensing classifiers can infer "privacy-critical information such as keyboard typing, gesture recognition and activity tracking" — [Restuccia, "IEEE 802.11bf: Toward Ubiquitous Wi-Fi Sensing", arXiv 2103.14918 (2021)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.14918)

ISP-operated sensing: Comcast Xfinity WiFi Motion
- Comcast's WiFi Motion detects changes in RF signals between the gateway and WiFi-connected devices (e.g. a TV or printer) and pushes notifications; coverage in 2026 describes the rollout as reaching "tens of millions" of routers — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/comcast-xfinity-shield-wifi-motion-privacy); [BleepingComputer](https://bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/comcast-turns-your-xfinity-wifi-into-a-home-motion-detector); [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/millions-of-comcast-wifi-routers-can-now-tell-when-youre-moving-around-the-house-2000800324)
- Disclosure term, as quoted by Schneier and others from Comcast's support page: "Comcast may disclose information generated by your WiFi Motion to third parties without further notice to you in connection with any law enforcement investigation or proceeding, any dispute to which Comcast is a party, or pursuant to a court order or subpoena." — [Schneier on Security, Sept 2026](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2026/09/wireless-routers-as-motion-detectors.html); [Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/privacy/comcast-xfinity-shield-routers-motion-sensors/); primary page: [Xfinity support, WiFi Motion](https://www.xfinity.com/support/articles/wifi-motion) (not directly verified)
- Critics note Comcast does not specify what information, or which third parties beyond law enforcement, may be involved — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/comcast-xfinity-shield-wifi-motion-privacy)
- Mitigating facts: the feature is off by default / opt-in via the Xfinity app; Comcast says it does not monitor the motion or notifications; it does not identify who is moving or their exact location. Turning off notifications alone may not stop detection, and disabling the feature reportedly does not delete previously collected timestamped motion logs — [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/networking/routers/new-xfinity-router-motion-detecting-feature-stokes-privacy-fears-feature-powered-by-wi-fi-signals); [All About Cookies](https://allaboutcookies.org/comcast-motion-sensor-routers); [Kaspersky blog](https://kaspersky.com/blog/wifi-sensing-motion-detection-howto/53851)
- Opinion is split: TechRadar calls it a potential "privacy nightmare"; other coverage (including a CNET writer cited in the coverage) argues presence sensing is less invasive than a camera and that the feature alone is probably not a huge privacy problem — [TechRadar](https://techradar.com/computing/comcast-is-adding-motion-detection-to-millions-of-routers-and-im-worried-its-a-privacy-nightmare); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/comcast-xfinity-shield-wifi-motion-privacy)
- Commentary raises the landlord scenario: whoever controls a building's mesh network controls a building-wide presence sensor (blog opinion, not research) — [DEV Community / Haven Messenger](https://dev.to/havenmessenger/wi-fi-sensing-your-router-is-becoming-a-motion-detector-2i0a)

IEEE 802.11bf debate
- IEEE Std 802.11bf-2025 (Amendment 4, WLAN sensing) was approved 28 May 2025 and published 26 Sept 2025 — [IEEE Standards Association record](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/12207/10365/)
- Pre-publication overviews split the risk into (a) overhearing sensing measurement reports and (b) overhearing sensing packets/signals; mitigations discussed include encrypted measurement reports and protected LTF sequences borrowed from 802.11az — [An Overview on IEEE 802.11bf, arXiv 2207.04859](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.04859)
- Restuccia (2021) argued individuals "should be provided the opportunity to opt out of SENS services"; commentators quoted the concern that security/privacy controls were "still to be worked out" — [arXiv 2103.14918](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.14918); [Schneier, Apr 2021](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2021/04/wi-fi-devices-as-physical-object-sensors.html); [Private Internet Access blog](https://privateinternetaccess.com/blog/how-a-30-year-old-technology-wifi-will-turn-into-our-next-big-privacy-problem)
- A Nov 2025 systematisation-of-knowledge paper on WiFi CSI biometrics still lists privacy/anonymisation as open challenges — [SoK, arXiv 2511.11381](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.11381)
- A 2026 arXiv paper states 802.11bf embeds features such as MAC address randomisation and secure session keys (single secondary source, unverified against the standard text). Privacy-specific MAC enhancements are being handled in a separate amendment, P802.11bi — [Towards Lawful ISAC in Cellular Networks, arXiv 2608.18902](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18902); [IEEE SA listing](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/1641/10365/)

### Inferences
- The "privacy-preserving alternative to cameras" claim is true in a narrow sense (no images) but increasingly weak: 2025 research shows the same signals can be biometric identifiers, and they are collectable by parties outside the home without the occupant's knowledge.
- For a self-built system, the privacy risk profile differs sharply by architecture: a locally processed motion/presence detector is much lower risk than one that trains per-person identification models or ships CSI/BFI or event logs to a cloud provider or ISP whose terms permit third-party disclosure.
- People affected who never consented include guests, domestic workers, and (through walls) neighbours; they cannot see or detect the sensing, unlike a visible camera.

### Gaps
- No EFF statement specifically on WiFi sensing was found in searches; I cannot say what EFF has or has not published. Civil-society critique located was mainly Schneier, privacy-focused media, and academic authors.
- No data protection authority (EDPB, ICO, FTC) guidance specific to CSI-based sensing was found.
- No figures found on how many law-enforcement requests Comcast has received for WiFi Motion data, nor on the retention period of motion logs.
- Whether the published 802.11bf-2025 text includes an opt-out or mandatory protection of sensing reports was not verified against the standard.

## 2. What laws are relevant (US, EU/UK), and is sensing in your own home lawful?

### Takeaway
No statute or court ruling found addresses CSI-based WiFi sensing specifically; legality has to be reasoned by analogy to home-camera, wiretap, biometric and data-protection law. Sensing confined to your own home for personal purposes is generally treated like domestic CCTV (lawful, largely outside GDPR), but the position changes once sensing reaches neighbours or shared spaces, identifies individuals (biometric-like data), covers workers in the home, or involves transmitting interference such as jamming or deauthentication.

### Cited Findings

United States
- Fourth Amendment (government sensing only): Kyllo v. United States (2001) held 5-4 that thermal imaging of a home was a search; the test turns on whether sense-enhancing technology is "in general public use". The Court did not define that phrase, and the majority record referenced emerging radar- and ultrasound-based through-the-wall systems — [Washington University Law Quarterly (Seamon)](https://journals.library.wustl.edu/lawreview/article/2454/galley/19287/view); [Criminal Law Practitioner, "Revisiting Kyllo"](https://www.crimlawpractitioner.org/post/revisiting-kyllo-v-united-states-publicly-available-technology-test-and-it-s-implication-on-drone)
- A blog commentary argues the "general public use" prong may cut against protection for WiFi sensing because WiFi hardware is the most widely deployed radio equipment in history (opinion, not scholarship) — [DEV Community / Haven Messenger](https://dev.to/havenmessenger/wi-fi-sensing-your-router-is-becoming-a-motion-detector-2i0a)
- Wiretap Act: Joffe v. Google (9th Cir. 2013) held that capturing payload data from unencrypted WiFi networks is covered by the Wiretap Act and does not fall within the "readily accessible to the general public" exception. The case concerned payload content, not signal-level measurements — [Joffe v. Google, Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joffe_v._Google,_Inc.); [EPIC case page](https://epic.org/documents/ben-joffe-v-google/); [FindLaw opinion](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/us-9th-circuit/1643851.html)
- FCC / interference: WiFi blocking by deauthentication violates Section 333 of the Communications Act; Marriott paid a $600,000 civil penalty (2014) for sending de-authentication packets against guests' hotspots, and the FCC's 2015 enforcement advisory states that operating, marketing or selling any jamming equipment is illegal — [FCC Enforcement Advisory DA 15-113](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-15-113A1_Rcd.pdf); [FCC news release](https://transition.fcc.gov/bureaus/eb/News_Releases/DOC-329743A1.html); [Kelley Drye analysis](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/commlaw-monitor/more-enforcement-action-at-the-fcc-enforcement-bureau-issues-600000-penalty-for-wi-fi-blocking)
- State biometric law, Illinois BIPA: the "biometric identifier" definition is a closed list (retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, scan of hand or face geometry) with no residual clause; gait is not listed, and courts require that the data be able to identify an individual — [Morrison Foerster, "Getting BIPA Right"](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240503-getting-bipa-right-biometric-identifiers-must-identify); [Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202407/scope-and-contours-of-bipa-biometric-identifiers-and-information)
- California CCPA: the definition of biometric information is broader and expressly includes "gait patterns or rhythms" and keystroke patterns that can establish identity; Washington's My Health My Data Act likewise names gait patterns that constitute identifying information — [Troutman Pepper](https://www.troutman.com/insights/are-you-ready-for-the-bipa-tsunami-the-new-wave-of-biometric-statutes/); [BCLP, MHMDA FAQ](https://www.bclplaw.com/en-US/events-insights-news/washington-my-health-data-act-faqs-processing-biometric-data.html)
- Home surveillance of workers and guests (camera analogy): sources state that video-only cameras in one's own home to monitor a nanny are generally legal in all 50 states; audio recording triggers state wiretap / all-party-consent rules (e.g. California); and bathrooms and a live-in worker's own bedroom are off limits and can constitute criminal voyeurism or unlawful surveillance regardless of who owns the home. Sources disagree on which states require notice — [SafeWise](https://safewise.com/are-nanny-cams-legal); [Recording Law](https://www.recordinglaw.com/us-laws/surveillance-camera-laws/nanny-cam-laws/); [LegalMatch](https://legalmatch.com/law-library/article/babysitternanny-camera-legality.html)

EU / UK
- Household exemption: in Ryneš the CJEU held a home camera covering a public path and a neighbour's property fell outside the purely personal/household exemption — [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/cctv-cjeu-narrows-the-scope-of-the-household-exemption)
- UK: cameras capturing only the homeowner's own property generally fall within the domestic-purposes carve-out; capturing people beyond the property boundary (public areas, neighbours, communal spaces) brings UK GDPR and the DPA into play. gov.uk guidance was updated 21 May 2026; ICO domestic CCTV guidance is under review following the Data (Use and Access) Act (law from 19 June 2025) — [gov.uk domestic CCTV guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/domestic-cctv-using-cctv-systems-on-your-property/domestic-cctv-using-cctv-systems-on-your-property); [ICO domestic CCTV guidance](https://ico.org.uk/your-data-matters/domestic-cctv-systems-guidance-for-people-using-cctv/)
- UK: if the householder acts as an employer (e.g. of domestic staff), the activity is not purely domestic and data protection law applies as an employer, per ICO material — [ICO disclosure document](https://ico.org.uk/media2/fwto2vqw/ic-382311-s8d1-disclosure.pdf)
- Ireland: DPC says the domestic exemption likely applies while capture stays within the perimeter of one's own home for personal purposes; cameras should "under no circumstances" view neighbours' homes or gardens; most complaints concern neighbours' CCTV or smart doorbells — [DPC domestic CCTV guidance](https://www.dataprotection.ie/sites/default/files/uploads/2026-05/Domestic_CCTV_A5_ENG_Final.pdf); [DPC case studies](https://dataprotection.ie/en/dpc-guidance/case-studies/cctv/domestic-cctv)
- Closest regulator guidance on WiFi-derived tracking: Spain's AEPD (with regional authorities, 2024) treats WiFi tracking of presence and movement as processing of personal data requiring an Article 6(1) lawful basis, necessity and proportionality; it notes data can be personal even when the controller does not realise it. This concerns device-based tracking, not device-free CSI sensing — [AEPD guidance](https://www.aepd.es/guides/wi-fi-tracking-technologies-guidance-for-data-controllers.pdf); [DataGuidance summary](https://www.dataguidance.com/news/spain-aepd-publishes-guidance-processing-personal-data)
- A 2026 preprint on integrated sensing argues always-on RF sensing collects biometric and spatial data without a defined purpose, in tension with GDPR data minimisation, and points to AI Act limits on inferring characteristics from biometric data — [arXiv 2605.07328](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.07328)
- EU AI Act prohibited practices relevant by analogy: biometric categorisation inferring sensitive characteristics; emotion inference in workplaces and education (medical/safety exception); real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement. Other remote biometric identification uses are classed as high-risk — [Bundesnetzagentur, prohibited practices](https://www.bundesnetzagentur.de/EN/Areas/Digitalisation/AI/08_ProhibitedPractices/start.html); [CMS on Commission guidelines](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/eu-commission-issues-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices-part-ii); [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-the-eu-ai-act-understanding-prohibited-ai-practices-and-their-interplay-with-the-gdpr-dsa/)

### Inferences
- Own home, own devices, motion/presence only, processed for personal purposes: by analogy to domestic CCTV this is generally lawful in the US and sits within the household carve-out in the EU/UK. This is an inference from camera law, since no WiFi-sensing-specific authority was found.
- The lines most likely to matter, in rough order of risk:
  - Sensing that reaches into a neighbour's unit or shared corridor. RF does not stop at a wall, so in apartments and terraced housing the Ryneš/ICO "beyond your boundary" logic could remove the household exemption, and unlike a camera the field of view cannot simply be re-aimed.
  - Person identification. Training a per-person gait/CSI model produces data that looks like biometric information under CCPA-style definitions and plausibly GDPR biometric data when used to uniquely identify; BIPA's closed list makes its application to RF gait signatures doubtful.
  - Workers in the home (nannies, cleaners, carers). The householder may be treated as an employer, which takes the activity out of the purely domestic sphere in the UK/EU; notice is the prudent minimum everywhere.
  - Private spaces. Bathrooms and a live-in worker's or lodger's bedroom are where camera law imposes criminal liability; pose- or activity-level sensing there is the highest-risk configuration even if no image is produced.
  - Tenants. A landlord sensing inside a tenant's unit is not sensing "their own home"; the household-exemption and own-property reasoning does not carry over.
  - Active radio measures. Any defence or test that jams or deauthenticates other people's devices is unlawful in the US irrespective of motive.
- Passive capture of unencrypted BFI or frame timing from a neighbour's network is legally murkier than capturing payload: Joffe covered payload content, and whether signal-level measurements are "contents" of a communication is unresolved in the sources found.
- Kyllo constrains police, not private householders, but it matters indirectly: data a householder or vendor stores can be obtained by subpoena or court order (as Comcast's terms make explicit), which sidesteps the question of whether police could sense the home directly.
- Most individual consumers are not "businesses" under CCPA or controllers under GDPR for purely domestic use, but a commercial vendor or cloud service processing the data is.

### Gaps
- No law review article or court decision applying Kyllo, the Wiretap Act, BIPA or GDPR Article 9 specifically to CSI/BFI-based sensing was found; whether raw CSI is "biometric data" under GDPR remains open in the sources.
- FCC Part 15 treatment of monitor-mode reception and CSI extraction, and of modified firmware on certified devices, was not sourced; no finding either way.
- The AI Act's exclusion for natural persons using AI in purely personal, non-professional activity (believed to be in Article 2) was not confirmed from a source and should be checked against Regulation (EU) 2024/1689.
- ePrivacy Directive applicability to device-free sensing was not found in any source.
- US landlord-tenant and guest-specific rules for in-unit sensing were not covered by the sources; state lists of notice requirements conflict between sources.
- Jurisdictions beyond US/EU/UK (e.g. Canada, Australia, China) were not researched.

## 3. What are the security weaknesses of WiFi sensing as an intrusion detector, and what defenses exist?

### Takeaway
WiFi sensing is a weak primary security control: published attacks can suppress or falsify detections through signal perturbation and interference, classic approaches lose accuracy for slow movement, vendors themselves disclaim accuracy, and the whole system depends on WiFi and power staying up. It is best treated as a supplementary layer alongside conventional sensors; independent, real-world reliability data for consumer products is largely missing.

### Cited Findings

Attacks on the sensing pipeline
- WiIntruder (arXiv, 2024): a black-box perturbation attack on WiFi sensing using GAN-generated surrogates, designed to be stealthy — [arXiv 2404.15587](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15587v1)
- Physical-layer attacks that manipulate WiFi packet preambles to alter CSI reported success rates of 90.47% against activity recognition and 83.83% against user authentication — [NJIT/Rutgers, "Practical Adversarial Attack on WiFi Sensing Through Unnoticeable..."](https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/practical-adversarial-attack-on-wifi-sensing-through-unnoticeable/); [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511800)
- Evasion attacks on deep-learning WiFi gesture recognition (IEEE Transactions on Mobile Computing, 2025) reached 97.0% (white-box) and 95.6% (black-box) attack success with an undetectable power change, on the SignFi and Widar datasets — [University of Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3191078/)
- Interference: inducing CSI losses with interference signals can disrupt WiFi behaviour recognition, and cross-technology interference can be harder to detect than conventional jamming — [NSF PAR 10296543](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10296543)
- A 2024 survey separates spoofing attacks (physical/signal layer) from adversarial attacks (algorithm layer) and frames wireless sensing as victim, weapon and shield — [arXiv 2412.03064](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.03064)
- Deauthentication: Protected Management Frames (mandatory in WPA3, optional in WPA2) block classic deauth injection, but many WPA2 clients lack it; research shows deauthentication remains feasible against some PMF-enabled WPA2/WPA3 networks, and a 2026 paper reports channel-switch-announcement spoofing that bypasses PMF. PMF does nothing against RF jamming — [arXiv 2602.23513](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23513v1); [KU Leuven paper](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/667987); [mrn-cciew, Dec 2025](https://mrncciew.com/2025/12/22/how-to-stop-wi-fi-deauth-attacks-wpa3/)

Evasion and reliability
- Detection accuracy of earlier WLAN intrusion-detection approaches "declines significantly" when a person moves very slowly; later work proposed HMM-based classifiers and phase (rather than amplitude) features, which are more sensitive to slow motion — [Stevens Institute, "Robust WLAN-Based Indoor Intrusion Detection Using PHY Layer Information" (IEEE Access 2017)](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/publications/robust-wlan-based-indoor-intrusion-detection-using-phy-layer-info/); [GLOBECOM 2016 paper](https://cis.temple.edu/%7Exjdu/Papers_Du/2016_GLOBECOM_Lv.pdf); [Sensors 2015 (PMC4721815)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4721815)
- Academic prototypes report high detection and low false-positive rates in tested rooms (e.g. Wi-Alarm), but these are controlled evaluations — [Wi-Alarm, Sensors 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6566976/)
- Comcast states that home size, layout, building materials and placement of the router and devices affect WiFi Motion's ability to detect motion and that it does not guarantee performance; when motion is detected the system cannot say what moved or exactly where — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/comcast-xfinity-shield-wifi-motion-privacy); [Kaspersky blog](https://kaspersky.com/blog/wifi-sensing-motion-detection-howto/53851)
- Evidence that WiFi sensing reduces false alarms comes mainly from vendors (e.g. an Origin AI executive's commentary); no independent testing of pet tolerance or false-alarm rates was found — [SecurityInfoWatch](https://www.securityinfowatch.com/residential-technologies/article/53073404/residential-technologies)
- Baseline for comparison: false alarms are a general problem in residential security (62% of system owners reported "too many" false alarms in the prior 12 months, Parks Associates data), and conventional PIR sensors have known pet-immunity limits (e.g. up to about 55 lb, weaker with multiple pets) — [SecurityInfoWatch](https://www.securityinfowatch.com/residential-technologies/article/53073404/residential-technologies); [ADT](https://www.adt.com/resources/motion-sensors-pets)
- Dual-technology (PIR + microwave) sensors cut false alarms only when both must trigger; the two technologies fail in different conditions (sunlight fools PIR, movement behind a partition fools microwave) — [IPVM PIR selection guide](https://ipvm.com/reports/pir-detector-selection-guide)

Defenses and privacy-preserving countermeasures
- IRShield (Ruhr University Bochum / MPI, 2021; presented for IEEE S&P 2022 per the authors' listing): an intelligent reflecting surface with randomised configurations obfuscates the wireless channel as a plug-and-play add-on; it cut a state-of-the-art human-motion-detection attack to 5% or lower detection rates — [arXiv 2112.01967](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.01967); [CASA RUB publication page](https://casa.rub.de/forschung/publikationen/detail/irshield-a-countermeasure-against-adversarial-physical-layer-wireless-sensing)
- Limits of IRShield: a 2025 paper showed an attacker with its own metasurface near the receiver could partly restore motion detection, and protection weakened when the defending surface faced away from the adversary — [The Battle of Metasurfaces, arXiv 2511.13939](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.13939)
- Cover traffic: the "Et Tu Alexa" authors proposed having the access point inject cover traffic on behalf of each device to confuse outside sniffers, having judged MAC randomisation and rate limiting impractical or ineffective — [arXiv 1810.10109](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.10109v4)
- BFI obfuscation: the LeakyBeam authors propose obfuscating beamforming feedback packets with minimal hardware changes — [NDSS 2025 paper page](https://ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/lend-me-your-beam-privacy-implications-of-plaintext-beamforming-feedback-in-wifi)
- Model-side defenses: NoiFi (an attack-agnostic detector for off-manifold adversarial inputs) and a Nov 2025 benchmark of adversarial robustness, including adversarial training, for CSI-based human sensing — [NoiFi paper](https://people.cs.vt.edu/~naren/papers/3779208.3806083.pdf); [arXiv 2511.20456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.20456v1)
- Wi-Peep mitigation proposed by its authors: chip makers adding randomised variation to response timing; for users there is little to do beyond switching WiFi off — [University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/media/waterloo-researchers-discover-security-loophole-allowing)

### Inferences
- A WiFi-sensing alarm has a single shared failure mode that conventional wired or battery-backed sensors do not: cutting power or internet service, jamming 2.4/5 GHz, or deauthenticating the sensing clients disables detection across the whole home at once. A robust design should treat loss of the sensing link itself as an alarm condition and keep an independent path (wired contact sensors, battery backup, cellular signalling).
- Most published adversarial attacks target deep-learning classifiers (gesture, activity, authentication), not simple motion thresholds. A plain variance-based motion detector is less exposed to adversarial ML but more exposed to slow-movement evasion and to nuisance triggers.
- Sensitivity and nuisance alarms trade off: tuning down to ignore pets, fans or movement in an adjacent flat also makes slow or distant intruders easier to miss, and through-wall sensitivity means neighbours' movement can cause false alarms in multi-unit housing.
- Practical hardening that follows from the sources: WPA3 or WPA2 with PMF required, wired backhaul where possible, and using WiFi sensing as a corroborating signal next to door/window contacts and PIR rather than as the sole trigger.
- The physical-layer privacy defenses (IRShield, BFI obfuscation, cover traffic) are research prototypes or proposals; no evidence was found that any is available in consumer routers.

### Gaps
- No independent, peer-reviewed or lab comparison of consumer WiFi-sensing products against PIR/dual-tech/contact sensors (detection rate, false alarms, pet immunity) was found.
- No study was found quantifying false positives from fans, curtains, robot vacuums or pets for WiFi sensing.
- No documented real-world burglary in which a WiFi-sensing alarm was deliberately jammed or evaded was found; the attack literature is laboratory work.
- No recognised certification (e.g. alarm-grade standards) covering WiFi-sensing detectors was identified in the sources.

## 4. How can attackers use WiFi sensing against a home, and what does that imply?

### Takeaway
An outsider with cheap, passive equipment can already infer occupancy, movement and device locations inside a home from its normal WiFi traffic, without joining or decrypting the network. Adding more always-transmitting sensing devices increases that exposure, so a WiFi-sensing security system can leak the very information (is anyone home, where) that it is meant to protect.

### Cited Findings
- "Et Tu Alexa? When Commodity WiFi Devices Turn into Adversarial Motion Sensors" (UChicago / UCSB; NDSS 2020; arXiv Oct 2018): a single off-the-shelf receiver outside the property, measuring only signal strength and decoding no packets, can track people moving inside. Implemented on one commodity smartphone and tested in 11 real offices and residential apartments (31 WiFi devices, 5 volunteers). More WiFi devices inside make tracking more accurate — [arXiv 1810.10109](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.10109v4); [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/how-hackers-could-use-wi-fi-track-you-inside-your-home); [NDSS slides](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/23053-slides.pdf)
- LeakyBeam, "Lend Me Your Beam" (Zhejiang University, NTU, KAIST; NDSS Feb 2025): passive occupancy detection from plaintext beamforming feedback. At 20 m, true positive rate 82.7% and true negative rate 96.7%; plaintext BFI is present in 86% of WiFi 5/6 devices; client devices are unaffected, so the attack is hard to notice — [NDSS paper](https://ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2025-5-paper.pdf); [NDSS paper page](https://ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/lend-me-your-beam-privacy-implications-of-plaintext-beamforming-feedback-in-wifi)
- Wi-Peep (University of Waterloo, Abedi and Vasisht; MobiCom 2022): exploits "Polite WiFi" (devices acknowledge frames from any sender, even on password-protected networks) and time-of-flight to locate every WiFi device in a building to roughly one metre, through walls, from a drone carrying about $20 of hardware, in seconds — [University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/media/waterloo-researchers-discover-security-loophole-allowing); [TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/96552-polite-wifi-loophole-modified-drones-track-device-locations.html); [All About Circuits](https://www.allaboutcircuits.com/news/wi-peep-uses-wi-fi-enabled-drone-to-spy-through-walls/)
- BFId (CCS 2025) extends the outside-observer threat from occupancy to identity, again using unencrypted feedback and no special hardware — [KIT press release](https://www.kit.edu/kit/english/pi_2025_069_the-spy-who-came-in-from-the-wifi-beware-of-radio-network-surveillance.php)
- Keystroke/PIN inference (WiKey 2015, WindTalker 2016) shows fine-grained activity is recoverable under favourable conditions — [WindTalker paper](https://nsec.sjtu.edu.cn/publications/2016/When.pdf)

### Inferences
- Implication for system design: a DIY CSI system that keeps devices transmitting at a high, steady packet rate gives an outside observer a better illumination source and more anchors. The "more devices, more accurate" finding argues for the fewest sensing links that do the job, placed away from exterior walls, at the lowest workable transmit power.
- Implication for threat modelling: a burglar's most valuable inference is "nobody is home"; passive occupancy attacks with roughly 97% true-negative rate at 20 m (LeakyBeam) mean WiFi alone can answer that from the street. Encryption (WPA2/WPA3) does not help because these attacks use physical-layer or unencrypted management information.
- Implication for locating security hardware: Wi-Peep-style localisation could reveal where WiFi cameras, hubs and sensors sit, helping an intruder avoid or disable them; wired or non-WiFi devices are not exposed this way.
- The same asymmetry applies to the householder: your sensing can pick up neighbours, and theirs can pick up you.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found of these techniques being used in actual residential crime; all are research demonstrations.
- Effectiveness of the attacks against WiFi 6E/7 (6 GHz) deployments and whether newer chipsets randomise acknowledgement timing was not found.

## 5. Best-practice recommendations for ethical deployment

### Takeaway
No authoritative WiFi-sensing-specific code of practice was found; best practice has to be assembled from regulator guidance on domestic CCTV and WiFi tracking plus the research literature. The consistent themes are: keep sensing within your own boundary, tell people, prefer presence over identity, process locally, keep little and delete it, and do not rely on it alone.

### Cited Findings
- Regulators' domestic-CCTV position is to confine capture to your own property; capturing beyond the boundary brings data protection duties, and neighbours' homes and gardens should never be covered — [gov.uk domestic CCTV guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/domestic-cctv-using-cctv-systems-on-your-property/domestic-cctv-using-cctv-systems-on-your-property); [Irish DPC](https://www.dataprotection.ie/sites/default/files/uploads/2026-05/Domestic_CCTV_A5_ENG_Final.pdf)
- AEPD's WiFi-tracking guidance requires controllers to establish a lawful basis and show necessity and proportionality — [AEPD](https://www.aepd.es/guides/wi-fi-tracking-technologies-guidance-for-data-controllers.pdf)
- Researchers have argued people should be able to opt out of sensing services — [Restuccia, arXiv 2103.14918](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.14918)
- The WhoFi authors themselves flag the "legal implications of radio biometric profiling" — [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/novel-ai-system-whofi-identifies-people-using-only-wi-fi-signals/)
- Third-party-held sensing data can be disclosed to law enforcement or litigants without notice, and disabling a feature may not delete past logs (Comcast example) — [Schneier, Sept 2026](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2026/09/wireless-routers-as-motion-detectors.html); [All About Cookies](https://allaboutcookies.org/comcast-motion-sensor-routers)
- In the camera analogy, consent or notice to a household worker is what makes monitoring defensible, and private rooms are excluded — [LegalMatch](https://legalmatch.com/law-library/article/babysitternanny-camera-legality.html); [Recording Law](https://www.recordinglaw.com/us-laws/surveillance-camera-laws/nanny-cam-laws/)
- UK regulators treat a householder who employs staff as an employer for data protection purposes — [ICO disclosure document](https://ico.org.uk/media2/fwto2vqw/ic-382311-s8d1-disclosure.pdf)

### Inferences
(Recommendations below are synthesised from the cited findings; they are not taken from a single authoritative checklist.)
- Notice and consent: tell every household member, and tell guests, lodgers and domestic workers that WiFi-based motion sensing is in use, since they cannot see it. Get agreement from adults who live there. Offer a way to disarm or pause it while guests or workers are present where security allows.
- Purpose and capability limits: collect the least capable signal that meets the security goal. Whole-home or zone-level presence/motion is enough for intrusion detection; avoid pose estimation, per-person identification, vital-sign or sleep inference unless every affected person has agreed, because those push the data toward biometric and health categories.
- Spatial limits: calibrate and place links so detection zones stay inside your own unit; test whether movement next door or in a shared corridor triggers events and reduce sensitivity, power or placement if it does. Exclude bathrooms and guests' or workers' bedrooms.
- Local processing: run inference on-device or on a home server; do not upload raw CSI/BFI. If a cloud or ISP service is used, read its disclosure terms, because data held by a provider is reachable by subpoena or court order.
- Data minimisation and retention: store events (time, zone, armed state) rather than raw signal captures; set a short automatic retention window and delete on disarm where practical. Raw CSI archives are re-analysable later with better models, including for identification.
- Access control and security hygiene: restrict who can view logs and live status, since occupancy history is sensitive within a household too (it can enable monitoring of a partner or family member). Use WPA3 or PMF-required WPA2, keep firmware updated, and treat the sensing hub as a security-critical device.
- Honest reliability posture: present it as a supplementary layer, pair it with conventional sensors and an independent alert path, and alarm on loss of the sensing link.
- Radio conduct: use only certified equipment within regulatory limits and never deauthenticate or jam others' devices as a test or countermeasure.
- Revisit periodically: the legal classification of RF sensing data is unsettled and the capability of models is rising quickly (2025 identification results), so a deployment that is low-risk today can become higher-risk through a software update alone.

### Gaps
- No published best-practice framework specific to residential WiFi sensing from a regulator, standards body (IEEE, Wi-Fi Alliance, Connectivity Standards Alliance) or civil-society group was found in this research.
- No source was found on how consumer WiFi-sensing vendors other than Comcast (e.g. Origin AI partners, router makers, smart-plug/bulb sensing products) handle retention, local processing or law-enforcement requests.
